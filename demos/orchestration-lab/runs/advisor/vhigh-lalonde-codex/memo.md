# Adjudication: NSW treated versus CPS controls

The randomized NSW benchmark is $1,794.3: mean 1978 earnings among the 185 NSW treated units minus mean earnings among the 260 randomized NSW controls. Replacing the randomized controls with 15,992 CPS controls gives a raw difference of -$8,497.5. The unadjusted comparison is therefore severely biased.

The corrected specification table supports a deliberately narrow conclusion. Demographics-only adjustment fails badly: the nearest-neighbour estimate is -$2,797.9 and corrected no-trim five-stratum subclassification is -$4,046.1. Adding lagged earnings produces point estimates close to the experimental contrast specifically for 1:1 nearest-neighbour matching with replacement: $1,712.2 without the score-range restriction and $1,758.6 with it. The corresponding dependent gaps from the benchmark are -$82.2 (conditional 95% interval: [-$1,358.6, $1,194.2]) and -$35.8 ([-$1,311.4, $1,239.9]). These intervals are consistent with equality, but do not establish equivalence; a recovery claim would need a prespecified substantively meaningful equivalence tolerance.

Correcting the no-trim stratification matters. The earlier implementation used the treated-score minimum and maximum as `cut()` boundaries, so it silently discarded CPS controls outside that range. No-trim rows now use `-Inf`, the four internal treated-score quintiles, and `Inf`, making all 15,992 CPS controls eligible. With demographics plus lagged earnings, that corrected five-stratum estimate is -$180.7 (gap -$1,975.0, conditional 95% interval [-$2,883.2, -$1,066.8]), not the earlier $1,252.5 result. The trimmed version is $1,252.5, but it is a different support restriction and retains only 5,776 CPS controls. Thus coarse subclassification with the same richer score does not reproduce the benchmark; it also leaves maximum post-adjustment absolute standardized mean differences of .372 without trimming and .209 after trimming. The richer nearest-neighbour rows likewise have maximum absolute SMDs of about .37, so their favorable point estimates should not be presented as evidence of uniformly strong covariate balance.

All reported specifications retain all 185 NSW treated units. Consequently, trimming did **not** change the ATT target here; it only changed which CPS controls were eligible. Trimming can change the estimand in general, and if a revised design discarded treated units, comparison with the full-sample NSW benchmark would no longer be directly like-for-like. Sample-extrema score-range restriction is also a weak overlap diagnostic in a CPS pool of this size; the table therefore reports the effective CPS-control sample size as well as the number eligible/used.

The reported intervals are unique-unit fixed-weight HC calculations, conditional on the estimated score and realized matches or strata. For nearest-neighbour matching, repeated CPS use enters through each control's aggregate realized weight (its reuse count); these are not Abadie--Imbens analytic intervals and not full matching-estimator inference. The benchmark comparison uses an interval for the dependent gap. Because every row retains the same NSW treated units, their mean cancels exactly: the gap is the randomized NSW-control mean minus the weighted CPS-control mean. It is therefore inappropriate to treat ordinary estimate-level interval inclusion of the benchmark as a test of recovery.

A paper may report a specification-level replication: in this dataset, richer lagged-earnings 1-NN specifications yield point estimates near the randomized NSW contrast. It may not infer that propensity-score matching generally eliminates selection bias, robustly recovers the experimental effect, or makes treatment as-if random. The raw CPS comparison fails severely, demographics-only adjustment fails badly, and the favorable result is specification-dependent rather than a general property of propensity-score matching.
