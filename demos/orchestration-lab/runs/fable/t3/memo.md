# Re: Reviewer comment on baseline sensitivity

We thank the reviewer for pressing on this point — it prompted us to make the robustness of our headline result explicit rather than assumed, and the revised manuscript now reports the full baseline-sensitivity analysis below.

The reviewer is mechanically correct about multi-level attributes: level-versus-baseline AMCEs genuinely change when the reference category is relabeled. Total Daily Driving Time illustrates this in our data. Against the default 10-minute baseline, the 25/45/75-minute levels are −7.0, −14.1, and −23.7pp; re-anchored on the 75-minute baseline, the same data produce +23.7, +16.8, and +9.7pp. Every reported coefficient shifts. We now say so directly and report marginal means alongside AMCEs throughout.

That concern does not, however, extend to the crime finding, for a structural reason rather than an interpretive one. Violent Crime Rate is **binary**. It therefore has exactly one alternative baseline, and switching to it flips only the sign, not the magnitude: −25.1pp (IRR-corrected; −16.5pp uncorrected) becomes +25.1pp. For a two-level attribute the AMCE is, by construction, identical to the gap between the two marginal means — there is no residual baseline degree of freedom to exploit. The baseline-free marginal means make this concrete: respondents choose the lower-crime community 62.6% of the time versus 37.4% for the higher-crime community. That 25.1pp gap *is* the finding, and it references no category we were free to choose.

On "the ordering of attribute importance could change": ranking attributes by marginal-mean range (a quantity no baseline can touch) gives crime 25.1pp, driving time 23.7pp, housing cost 19.8pp, type of place 15.8pp, school quality 11.6pp, racial composition 5.9pp, and presidential vote 5.6pp. This ordering cannot change through relabeling. But we want to be precise, not just emphatic: crime leads driving time by only 1.4pp, and their confidence intervals overlap substantially, so crime's #1 rank is numerically ahead but not statistically resolved — we will not present it as a clean win.

Accordingly, the revised manuscript claims that violent crime rate is one of the strongest — the single largest observed — drivers of community choice, producing a swing of roughly 25pp (corrected; ~17pp uncorrected) that is robust to reference-category choice, comparable in magnitude to commute time, and clearly ahead of housing cost. We no longer describe crime as dominating the other attributes or as uniquely decisive, and we present the full marginal-mean table so readers can assess the ranking themselves.
