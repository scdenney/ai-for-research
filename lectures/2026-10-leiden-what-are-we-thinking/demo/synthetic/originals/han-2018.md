## The registers and what they count

Historians of return migration to Korea have leaned on one kind of source above all
others: the landing registers kept at Pusan from the autumn of 1945. The registers are
orderly, they are dated, and they give names, ages, and places of origin. They look like
a census of the movement. This chapter argues that they are nothing of the kind.

The registers were kept by the officials who met the official ships. A sailing arranged
by the occupation authorities in Japan arrived at a set pier, at a set hour, and its
passengers were counted, dusted for lice, and written down. That is the population the
registers describe.

## The boats people found

Most people who crossed did not wait for those ships. Official sailings carried a
minority of those who crossed. By one later estimate, perhaps one arrival in
three came on a ship the state had sent; the rest came on fishing boats and small
freighters hired for the night, landing on beaches and at minor harbours along the
coast where no clerk was waiting.

The reasons are not hard to find in the testimony. The official queue in Japan was long
and its baggage limits were strict. A family that sold its furniture and carried its
savings in cash had every reason to pay a boatman instead. The price of a crossing,
recorded in rice, cloth, and jewellery, rose and fell with the weather and with rumours
about the queue.

## Consequences for the historian

The registers record the ships the state sent, not the boats people found. A study that
takes the registers as its population will describe the most orderly third of the
movement and mistake it for the whole. Ages and origins in the registers skew toward
families who could afford to wait, and toward those whose papers were in order.

None of this makes the registers useless. They remain the best source for the
administration of return, for what the state intended and how it counted. They are a
poor source for the movement itself, and they should be read beside memoirs, local
newspapers, and the bureau's own monthly reports, which admit more uncertainty than the
registers do.
